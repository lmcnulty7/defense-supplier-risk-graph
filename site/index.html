The project in one minute

The imagined customer is an analyst who keeps military equipment maintained. Their question: "Which of our contracts depend on risky suppliers, and what breaks if one of them fails?"

What I didWhat I found
1. CollectDownloaded every DoD maintenance and repair contract for 2023-2025 from USAspending.gov (the government's public spending database), plus subcontracts and 187,320 contract changes. 57,596 contracts worth $225.2B, paid to 11,658 companies.
2. MatchThe same company is spelled differently across records, and some records lack its ID number. I built a matcher that decides which records are the same company. An easy test said it was over 90% accurate. A realistic test said 56%. I found why and raised it to 83%.
3. ConnectBuilt a map (a "knowledge graph") linking each company to its contracts, its owner, the offices that pay it and the countries where the work happens. Flagged companies that look risky. Every question is answered two independent ways, and both always agree.
4. Test the flagsChecked whether flagged companies actually had contracts ended early the following year.Not more than their size explains. The flags show how much is at stake, not how likely a problem is.
5. AskBuilt an AI assistant (Claude) that can only answer by looking things up in the map, and must cite a record ID for every fact. Tested it with known-answer and trick questions. Real questions: answered correctly. Weak spot: company names that are almost right. Fixed in the lookup tool.

The pattern: each part looked fine on an easy test. A realistic test found the real problem, which I then fixed or reported.

Step 2: Working out which records are the same company

"Boeing Co" and "The Boeing Company" are one company. So are a firm and its new name after an acquisition. Records with the government ID are easy to group; for records without it, a matcher compares names and addresses.

Easy test: show the matcher two records and ask "same company?" It scored over 90%.

Realistic test: take 620 real records of companies that were renamed, hide their ID, and ask the system to find the right company among 11,158. The original matcher got only 56%.

Why: to save time, the matcher only compared records with similar names. A renamed company has a different name but usually the same address, so it was never compared.

Fix: also compare records at the same address, and learn from the data how much each clue (name, address, ZIP, city, state) should count. Result: 83%, with fewer wrong matches.

Then fewer false matches: companies that aren't in the list at all were sometimes matched to a neighbour at the same address. Requiring a clearer winner, chosen on half the records and checked on the other half, cut those false matches from 8.7% to 4.4%, for 5 fewer points of correct matches.

Lesson: test the way the system will actually be used.

Technical detail (for follow-up questions)
End to end: correct links 55.8% (original) → 82.7% (learned + address check, dev-tuned threshold). Shipped operating point: threshold 0.70, margin 0.20, chosen on the tuning half to maximise correct − 2 × false merges; on the confirmation half absent false links 8.7% → 4.4%, wrong links 3.8% → 0.6%, correct 83.6% → 78.5%. Pairwise test split, learned weights: precision 0.949 / recall 0.909 on identifier-derived pairs; acronym recall 0.10 → 1.00. Labels are automatic: pairs sharing an ID, look-alike pairs with different IDs, and synthetic misspellings. Learned weights are a logistic regression on agreement evidence, chosen over hand-set weights on the dev split. Wrong links 5.32% → 3.55%. Cost: companies not in the index falsely linked 7.0% → 8.4%. Disclosure: the address check was added after diagnosing these same records; it has no tunable setting.

Step 3: A map of who depends on whom

Each company, parent company, contract, office, country and product category is a point on the map. Links say who got which contract, who subcontracts to whom, who owns whom, who paid, and where the work was done. Every point and link records which download it came from.

Ten standard questions are pre-written ("which offices depend on this supplier?", "what is its blast radius?" and so on). Each is computed two independent ways, and the tests fail if the answers ever differ.

I also loaded the map into Neo4j, a standard graph database, and checked it gives exactly the same results.

Step 4: Do my risk flags predict trouble? I checked against what happened

Public records show when the government ends a contract early (a termination). Using only data up to one year, each method ranks suppliers by how likely they are to have a termination the next year; then I check against what actually happened (5,541 suppliers, 145 had one).

How well each method ranks everyone (0.5 = coin flip, 1 = perfect)

How good the top of the list is (dashed line = random picks)

Result: simply counting a supplier's contracts (0.76) beats my flags (0.64). Big suppliers have more contracts, so more chances for one to be ended. A trained model (0.77) barely beats counting overall, but is clearly better at the top of the list.

Lines are 95% ranges: if two overlap a lot, the difference may be luck.

Technical detail (for follow-up questions)
Outcome: any termination (default, cause, convenience) or cancellation in the next fiscal year. Train on FY2022-FY2023 features, test on FY2024 features → FY2025 outcomes. Left chart ROC-AUC, right chart average precision, with 1,000-resample bootstrap CIs. Model gain over size: AUC +0.013 (CI -0.013 to +0.039, not significant); average precision +0.070 (CI +0.028 to +0.110, significant). An earlier split and a run without the dominant office agree.

How the likelihood score is predicted

  • What it predicts: the chance a supplier has a contract terminated or cancelled next fiscal year (a proxy for disruption, not proof of supplier failure).
  • How: a logistic regression on that year's contract activity (size, churn, past terminations, competition), trained on FY2022-FY2023, tested on FY2024 features against FY2025 outcomes.
  • How well: precision in the top 5% riskiest is 17.7%, against a 2.6% base rate, 14.4% for size alone and 6.5% for the three flags.
  • What it means: most predictable risk is size, so the useful signal is excess_vs_size (riskier than size explains); the flags show impact, not likelihood, which is why the chart separates them.

Details: model card, backtest report.

Finding: the flags show what's at stake, not who will fail

How much more often flagged suppliers had a termination than unflagged ones. 1 = no difference; 2 = twice as often; 0.5 = half as often. "vs same-size firms" compares suppliers with a similar number of contracts.

  • Concentrated looked 3× riskier, but only because those suppliers are bigger. Against same-size firms, no difference.
  • Sole-source suppliers were less likely to have a termination: long-running maintenance deals with the original manufacturer are stable.
  • Overseas: the whole effect comes from one Navy office in Japan, which ends many contracts for its own administrative reasons (17%-38% of each year's terminations). Without it, no difference.

So what: a flag tells you how bad a failure would be. "How likely" needs its own separate score.

Step 5: The AI assistant, and testing it fairly

You type a question. Claude can't search freely: it can only use five lookup tools (find a company by name, run one of the ten pre-written questions, test what happens if suppliers fail, get a country's risk, get a contract's details). Every fact must cite a record ID, or it must say the data can't answer.

Testing: questions whose correct answers are computed from the data, plus trick questions about companies that don't exist. The first test was too easy (87/88 right, 13/13 tricks declined), so I wrote a harder one with comparisons, rankings and look-alike names.

  • Without the lookup tools, Claude answers nothing (0/44). It declines rather than making things up, but it can't help. The map is what makes it useful.
  • With the tools, even the small model gets nearly every real question right (43/44, 44/44, 43/44), at about 1/52 of the large model's cost.
  • Most of the small model's mistakes were on trick questions (its other misses: c_what_if_top_alt). The next slide shows the tricks it fell for.

Black dots are separate runs (small model 3 times; large model once per set). All runs use the assistant's current instructions and tools. Cost per question: Large model + tools $0.0447 · Small model + tools $0.0009 · Small + tools + fix $0.0009 · Large model, no tools $0.0067.

The weak spot, and the fix: look-alike company names

The trick: ask about a company whose name is a real supplier's name with one word changed. The name lookup finds the real company as the closest match, and the small model often answered about it anyway, as if it were the company asked about.

The fix: the lookup now lists any words in the question that the matched name doesn't contain, and says not to swap companies silently. I changed the tool, not the instructions to the AI, so it works for any model.

Look-alike tricks caught (of 8): small model 4, 4, 4 → with the fix 8, 8, 8. Large model: 7. Real questions: still 44/44, 44/44, 44/44.

Technical detail (for follow-up questions)
Offline checks: the check fires on 8/8 look-alike tricks, 0/3 misspelled-but-correct names, 0.08% of synthetic same-company pairs, and 0 of 96 logged large-model lookups on real questions. The one trick the large model missed is arguably mislabelled (the matched company shares every other distinctive word, and the model said it was a likely, not certain, match); it is still counted as a miss.

Why these numbers can be trusted

  • The right answers are computed, not written by hand. Each one comes from the data and is re-checked a second, independent way. Questions where the two disagree are thrown out.
  • Answers are graded by code, not by opinion. An AI grader is used only for writing quality, and I tested it first by planting errors in real answers: it caught 100% of 40 obvious ones and 100% of 30 subtle ones (a count off by one, a percentage off by 5 points).
  • No hand-made labels. Test labels come from ID numbers, deliberate misspellings and the government's own termination codes.
  • Changes made after seeing results are disclosed. The harder question set was revised once after a trial run (and later extended with eight what-if questions for the scenario tool), and the address check was added after studying failures. Both are reported with before and after numbers.
  • Automatic checks. The test suite fails if any of these results gets worse.

Limits, and what I'd do with a real customer

Limits

  • Public data only: subcontracts are rarely reported, and there is no performance, financial or debarment data.
  • "Termination" is a stand-in for trouble. Most are for the government's convenience, not the supplier's fault, and one office accounts for a large share.
  • The question sets are small and built from templates. The large model ran the full sets once (two more runs would break the $10 budget); only its look-alike tricks were repeated (7 of 8 caught each time).
  • The country-risk feed is months old and barely varies between countries.

Next, with a customer

  • Sit with the analyst: which decisions would the two-score view change? Set thresholds to fit their work.
  • Add performance ratings, debarment lists and financial data, and re-test against supplier-fault outcomes.
  • Replace template questions with the analyst's real questions, keeping the two-way answer check.
  • Run on a production graph database, and track how often the name check fires.
